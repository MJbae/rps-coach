A mandatory extra keypress each round (about 400 ms) cut lose-shift from 77.48% to 69.80%, yet win rate was lowest in that condition: 33.5%, vs 35.1% with no pause and 37.3% when players chose their own pauses (variable vs fixed differed, F(2,70)=4.80, p=.011). Against a bot that exploited the player's own bias (Exp 2), the forced-pause win rate was also lowest: 31.3% vs 33.1% and 33.4%.
EFFECT: Win rate with the forced pause: -1.6 pp vs no pause (Exp 3) and -1.8 pp (Exp 2). Voluntary pause: +2.2 pp vs no pause (Exp 3). Lose-shift with the forced pause: -7.7 pp.
IMPLICATION: Do not expect pausing after a loss to win you more rounds by itself. It may make your next throw less predictable, which matters only against an opponent who exploits lose-shift; it does not help you read the opponent.
CAVEATS: Computer opponents and no performance pay. The forced pause also added an extra motor response. The Exp 3 bot did not punish lose-shift, so lower predictability could not pay off there.

[MF2] conf=medium
STATEMENT: Slowing down after a loss marks successful exploitation, but it has not been shown to cause it. Against exploitable bots, players with higher win-loss rates slowed more after losses than after wins (r=.647, .577 and .575 in Exps 1-3 of Dyson et al. 2018). Against random bots the usual speeding after losses appeared (Exps 2-3 pooled: wins 672 ms, losses 552 ms, draws 504 ms). The authors call the data 'correlational rather than causal'.
EFFECT: r of about .58-.65 between post-loss slowing and win-loss rate against exploitable bots. Against random bots, post-loss RT was 120 ms faster than post-win RT.
IMPLICATION: Players who have a working model of the opponent naturally slow down after an unexpected loss. Use the gap after a loss to recall the opponent's recent throws rather than simply waiting; MF1 shows that waiting alone did not help.
CAVEATS: Correlational, and reverse causation is plausible: for a player who is winning, losses are rare and draw attention (an orienting account discussed in Dyson 2024). Computer opponents.

[MF3] conf=medium
STATEMENT: In live play, choices track the opponent's previous gesture in a goal-directed way, and this sequential bias can masquerade as automatic imitation. In Aczel et al. 2015, sighted players paid to match tended to replay the opponent's previous gesture, and those paid to mismatch avoided it (three-way interaction OR=1.83, p<.001; same pattern in Belot's Oxford data, OR=1.64, p<.001). Blindfolded players repeated their own previous gesture mainly after wins (OR=1.14, p=.015). Once these biases were controlled, the remaining online-matching effect (match incentive, lead of at least 133 ms) shrank to OR=1.22 (p=.060), and a Bayes factor of 0.186 favoured no automatic imitation.
EFFECT: Odds ratios of 1.14-1.83 for history-dependent choices. The residual online-imitation effect is not significant once history is controlled.
IMPLICATION: Face-to-face, the usable information is the opponent's previous throw and its outcome, not the hand in motion. Track history (what they threw and whether they won); don't try to read or react to the hand at delivery.
CAVEATS: Two-gesture game, not RPS. Only the accepted manuscript was read. History effects are modest in size.

[MF4] conf=low
STATEMENT: In Ivan et al. 2018, mental load rather than speed drove the extra lose-shift. Adults doing serial subtraction and children aged 5-9 took longer per trial than unloaded adults (F(3,45)=4.37, p=.008; children d=1.28, loaded adults d=1.02), yet showed more lose-shift. Win counts did not differ (F(3,45)=1.61, p=.199). Within the loaded group, a longer gap after feedback reduced lose-shift (decay over 1-12 s).
EFFECT: d of about 1.0-1.3 for slower trials in the high-lose-shift groups; d=1.39-1.45 for their raised lose-shift.
IMPLICATION: Slowing your pace will not protect you if your attention is taken up. Lower your load by pre-deciding a simple plan and ignoring the crowd and the score. A distracted opponent may lose-shift even when moving slowly.
CAVEATS: Small groups, a two-option (not RPS) task, and fictitious money. The between-group link between slowness and lose-shift is confounded with load and age.
