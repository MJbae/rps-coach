In simultaneous team-against-team RPS, coordination within the team matters under both of Cho's rules once a team has 3 or more members, and the orientation of the split is what counts. With 2 per side under rule II (a team wins when only its members win), showing two different hands wins 0.583 against an independently random team. With 3 per side, the sweep's random-orientation 2+1 split gives 0.576 under rule II and 0.50 under rule I. The best composition is two members on a hand X and one on the hand X beats (Rock-Rock-Scissors, Paper-Paper-Rock or Scissors-Scissors-Paper), with X chosen at random. It wins 0.684 (rule II) and 0.6875 (rule I) against a random team, and 0.875 (rule II) or 0.50 (rule I) against a herding team. The reverse orientation (two on X, one on the hand that beats X) wins only 0.469 (rule II) or 0.3125 (rule I). With 4 per side, 3 on X plus 1 on the hand X beats wins 0.779 (rule II) and 0.859 (rule I). Herding on one hand gives 0.25 (rule II, 3v3) against random play. EFFECT: +8.3 percentage points (2v2) and +7.6 points (3v3) over 50% against random opponents; herding costs 16.7 and 25 points.
IMPLICATION: If the event uses the 'only one team has winners' rule, agree before each throw that no two teammates show the same hand and that the team never shows all three. CAVEATS: This is derived, not tested on people. It needs the rules to allow secret coordination, and it assumes opponents cannot predict which pair of hands you will use. Rule variants change the results.
RELIABILITY: Exact mathematics verified two ways (exact enumeration and simulation). No human data; assumes the opponent's throws are independent of ours.

At p = 0.50/0.52/0.55/0.60, a relay gives 0.500/0.537/0.593/0.683 for n=3, 0.500/0.549/0.621/0.733 for n=5 and 0.500/0.558/0.644/0.771 for n=7. STATEMENT: Derived result. In a winner-stays relay, the batting order does not change the team's win probability when bout probabilities follow a Bradley-Terry strength model, or when each player has the same edge against every opponent. Across 200 random 3-5-player cases the largest spread across orders was 4.4×10^-16. Order can matter when edges are matchup-specific. Example: one member has a 0.65 edge only against y1, and y1 leads the other team. With several matchup-specific edges, order can still matter even when the opposing order is unknown: in 3v3 with bout probabilities between 0.35 and 0.65, the effect is up to about 1.7 points, and with extreme edges about 18 points. IMPLICATION: Do not agonize over batting order unless you hold opponent-specific scouting and can see or predict the opposing order. If you have both, place the member with the matchup edge where they will face that opponent.
CAVEATS: Derived, not empirical. Assumes bout outcomes are independent of history; momentum and tilt are not modelled.
RELIABILITY: Exact mathematics. No empirical data on matchup-specific RPS edges.

STATEMENT: Across economics experiments, small groups that deliberate choose closer to game-theoretic predictions than individuals do (Charness & Sutter 2012; Kugler, Kausel & Kocher 2012). In beauty-contest games, groups were not smarter per se but learned faster, and outperformed individuals when the two competed (Kocher & Sutter 2005). With three-person teams (Sutter, Czermak & Feri 2013, N=192), Nash play was about 50% against about 40% for individuals (49.30% vs 40.97%), and the estimated share of strategic types was 62% against 40%. EFFECT: Nash play about +10 percentage points for teams; strategic-type share 62% against 40%.
IMPLICATION: Letting the team deliberate on a throw, or on the plan, should reduce obvious biases and make predictable opponents easier to exploit. In RPS against a random opponent, though, rationality guarantees only 1/3-1/3-1/3, not an edge.
CAVEATS: I read only the abstracts of three of the four sources. None tests RPS. The games studied are mostly dominance-solvable or social-preference games.

STATEMENT: Okano (2013, Games Econ. Behav. 77:168-180) studied repeated zero-sum games with a unique mixed equilibrium, the class RPS belongs to (RPS itself was not tested). Two-person teams that discussed each choice face to face matched minimax in O'Neill's 4×4 game at the decision-maker level, while individuals did not. The treatments were separate: 36 teams played other teams and 36 individuals played other individuals, over 15 practice and 150 paid rounds. Data were analysed through round 132 after a computer problem. A winning team earned 100 yen, split between its members, and a winning individual earned 50 yen. When teams played individuals, teams won above the equilibrium rate in one treatment but not the other. Teams' hide-and-seek play was less heterogeneous than individuals'. In a 5×5 Rapoport-Boebel game (Okano 2017 working paper; Osaka University, 2011-2012; separate treatments of 56 teams in 28 pairs and 40 individuals in 20 pairs; 120 rounds), hypothesis tests found teams consistent with minimax only in rounds 1-60. Model selection, however, found the minimax model fitted individuals' aggregate play best in both halves and teams' play in rounds 61-120. At the decision-maker level, minimax was the best model for about half of both teams and individuals. Runs tests rarely rejected serial independence for either group; for action C, 5.4% of teams and 10.0% of individuals over rounds 1-120.
EFFECT: Teams were statistically consistent with minimax while individuals were not (p-value based). Evidence of teams beating individuals is mixed (1 of 2 treatments).
IMPLICATION: A pair that jointly decides throws may keep its hand frequencies balanced and hard to exploit early on, but the benefit fades over long matches. For long sequences, use an external randomizer rather than relying on discussion.
CAVEATS: These are not RPS (4×4 and 5×5 games with unequal equilibrium mixes). Two of the three sources are working papers. The team advantage was not robust over time.
RELIABILITY: One peer-reviewed lab study (GEB) plus two working papers (one later published in AEL). Japanese students, modest yen incentives, games other than RPS, small samples.

STATEMENT: Cook et al. (2012, Proc R Soc B 279:780-786) found that seeing the opponent's hand while throwing produces involuntary imitation, not wins. In face-to-face play with one player blindfolded, draws rose to 36.3%, against 33.3% when both were blindfolded. Sighted players copied the blind player's Rock (b=0.245, p=0.034) and Scissors (b=0.266, p=0.006) but not Paper (b=0.086, n.s.). Sighted players won 31.3% of rounds and blind players 32.4%. Participants were 45 adults, and 14 triads were analysed; each triad played 9 matches of 20 rounds on an umpire's count of three.
EFFECT: Draws +3.0 points over 1/3; sighted players gained no win advantage (-1.1 points against the blind players).
IMPLICATION: In host-against-everyone games where a tie eliminates you (rule A), looking at the host's hand as you throw may make you copy it and raise your elimination rate. Commit to your pre-assigned hand (C7) and do not try to react to what you see.
CAVEATS: Tested in 1:1 play, not with one host and a crowd. Small sample (14 triads). The effect is modest.
RELIABILITY: Peer-reviewed lab study, small sample (14 triads), face-to-face, small stakes.

[team:C17] (confirmed)
STATEMENT: Towse et al. (2016, PLoS ONE 11(3):e0151306) found that when two people take turns building one 'random' digit sequence (digits 1-10), each avoids repeating the partner's last response. Immediate repetitions averaged 2.4 per 200 responses in real pair sequences, against 20.5 in composites of the same people's solo sequences and 19.8 expected by chance (Experiment 1, 40 undergraduates in 20 pairs). In Experiment 3 (33 Kyoto pairs), telling pairs to ignore their partner raised true-pair repetitions to 10.4 (cooperate: 1.5; composite: 21.5) but did not reach randomness ('neither makes you random').
EFFECT: Immediate repetitions in real pairs were about 12% of the chance level (2.4 against 19.8 per 200 responses).
IMPLICATION: Teammates who play in turn against the same host or opponent may unconsciously avoid the hand a teammate just used, which makes the team's sequence predictable. Use pre-generated random assignments (dice, a phone RNG) instead of reacting to teammates.
CAVEATS: The task was digit generation (10 options), not RPS (3 options). Applying it to RPS team play is an extrapolation.
RELIABILITY: Peer-reviewed lab study (PLoS ONE) with replication across countries, modest N, non-RPS task.

STATEMENT: Cho (2020; full text, which the sweep read only as an abstract) compares, for m players per side, the winner-stays relay (method I), simultaneous 'more winners wins' (II) and simultaneous 'only one team has winners' (III). Mean throws to a decision are 3.75 / 2.36 / 2.93 for m=2 and 6.19 / 4.49 / 5.78 for m=3; the exact values for m=3 are 6.19 / 4.48 / 5.80. In the relay, the number of decisive bouts N has P(N=i) = C(i-1,m-1)(1/2)^(i-1) for i=m..2m-1 (for m=3: 1/4, 3/8, 3/8). The relay is therefore a race to m bout wins, which independently confirms C9. Several printed variances are wrong: the relay values of 3.00 and 5.20 should be 39/16≈2.44 and 1143/256≈4.46, and method III for m=2 should be 631/196≈3.22, not 3.42.
EFFECT: A 3-a-side relay averages about 6.2 throws, against about 4.5-5.8 for simultaneous formats and 1.5 for 1v1.
IMPLICATION: A 3-person relay involves more throws, so more chances to observe the opponent's staying player, but also more time. Plan scouting and time budgets accordingly. The paper's means are reliable but its variances are not.
CAVEATS: Assumes uniform independent throws. The paper is framed around deciding which team starts first.

[MF5] conf=medium
STATEMENT: In Sutter, Czermak & Feri (2013), the teams' higher rate of Nash play (49.30% vs 40.97%, p<0.05) came from the 10 games in which one player had a dominant strategy. In the games where neither player had a dominant strategy, the class RPS belongs to, there was no significant difference between teams and individuals.
EFFECT: Overall +8.3 points of Nash play for teams; no significant difference in the non-dominance games.
IMPLICATION: Do not expect team deliberation in itself to improve RPS throws. The group-rationality advantage that C11 relies on is concentrated in games with dominant strategies. Spend team effort on structural levers instead: format-specific coordination (MF1, C7), randomisation devices, and shared scouting notes.
CAVEATS: One study. Its non-dominance games are not mixed-strategy games like RPS, and the lack of a difference may reflect low power.

[MF6] conf=medium
STATEMENT: Derived result. In simultaneous elimination among peers with no host (everyone throws, and when exactly two hands show, the winners continue until one player remains), a coordinated 3-person subgroup among 10 players makes the final survivor a teammate with probability 0.450 if it shows two on X and one on the hand X beats. The head-count share is 0.300; a random-orientation two-hand split gives 0.349, independent play 0.300 and herding on one hand 0.125. In 'pick one loser' games (losers continue until one remains), herding on one hand makes a teammate the final loser with probability only 0.125, against a 0.300 share. Namu Wiki describes this kind of collusion: when three of four players match hands, the fourth is effectively left in a 1:1 game. It suggests splitting into pairs as the countermeasure.
EFFECT: Winner mode: +15 points (0.450 vs 0.300). Loser mode: -17.5 points of exposure (0.125 vs 0.300).
IMPLICATION: The right coordination depends on the objective. If the format rewards the last survivor, use the 2+1 split where X beats the single hand. If it punishes the last loser, herd on one hand. Check whether such coordination against non-team players is allowed.
CAVEATS: Derived, not tested with people. Organisers may ban collusion or neutralise it by splitting players into pairs. Other teams coordinating would erode the gain.
