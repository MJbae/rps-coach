- No significant score losses.

[MF2] conf=medium
STATEMENT: **Human RPS players tend to over-repeat, not over-switch.** In the largest human-vs-human RPS lab study (360 paid students, groups of 6, 300 rounds), players were more likely to repeat their previous hand than to shift to either other hand, which implies a repeat rate above 1/3.
- The effect is strongest at the lowest win payoff and 'still very significant even at a = 100'.
- When players did change hand, clockwise and counter-clockwise shifts were about equally likely.
- This contradicts the over-switching assumption carried over from tennis serves and random-generation tasks in C09 and C10.
EFFECT: - Direction only; the values are in figures.
- Repeat > each shift implies r > 1/3.
- Against a player with r=0.45, throwing what beats their last hand earns (3r−1)/2 = +0.175 per throw.
IMPLICATION: - Do not assume opponents over-switch; tally each opponent's stay/up/down counts.
- If repeats exceed about 40%, throw what beats their last hand.
- If repeats fall below about 25%, throw what their last hand beats.
CAVEATS: - Chinese students in a lab, with random re-pairing.
- Dyson 2016 (against a computer) found more switching after losses and draws.
- No data from tournaments or Korea.

[MF3] conf=medium
STATEMENT: **Scoring by match results favours unexploitable designs.** In Billings's 1999 'Best of the Best' division (match result only, with progressive elimination), defensive programs beat stronger margin-maximizers:
- A fixed De Bruijn sequence finished 3rd, losing only to Iocaine Powder and Phasenbott; it was 28th on points.

[MF4] conf=medium
STATEMENT: **Two failure modes of pattern-hunting** (Billings's dummy bots):
- **Decoys:** a statistically balanced De Bruijn sequence 'naturally gives the appearance of certain tendencies', so over-zealous exploiters are 'continuously deceived and punished'. The stronger the pattern detector, the more susceptible it is.
- **Missing absences:** simple habits defined by the absence of a pattern are missed by sophisticated predictors. Against 'Always Switchin'' (never repeats; best counter +500 per 1,000), even some of the strongest programs managed only +350. Against Switch A Lot (12% repeats; maximum 320), the simple Anti-rotn bot, which counts only repeat/up/down rotations, 'scored close to the maximum', while Iocaine Powder and Phasenbott scored under +100.
EFFECT: Simple stay/up/down tracking earned about 3× more against Switch A Lot than the top general-purpose predictors.
IMPLICATION: - Have a teammate tally each opponent's stay/up/down counts; that simple statistic catches habits that intuition misses.
- In short matches, do not chase apparent streaks: balanced-looking sequences can bait you.
CAVEATS: Bots in long matches; humans read patterns differently.

[MF5] conf=medium
STATEMENT: **Salience-driven choices are costly in zero-sum games.** In Rubinstein–Tversky hide-and-seek (four boxes, six experiments), seekers found hiders 32% of the time, against the 25% that the equilibrium mix guarantees. Seekers chose the 'least salient' central box even more often (46%) than hiders did (37%). A level-k model with a uniformly random L0 coincides with equilibrium, so such deviations require a salient anchor.
EFFECT: +7 pp detection rate (32% vs 25%) against hiders who followed the salient pattern.
IMPLICATION: - Any choice driven by an anchor that opponents can also guess creates an exploitable tilt. Examples: 'Rock is obvious', 'don't repeat', 'beat their favourite'.
- Edges arise only from salience. Without a read, use a device.
CAVEATS: Not RPS; the figures come via a review, not the original paper.

[MF6] conf=high
STATEMENT: **Most experienced Roshambull players show no frequency bias; their exploitable structure is in reactions.**
- Only 18% of experienced players (≥100 matches; n=7,758) had throw distributions significantly different from uniform (20% for their k0 play).
- About half (47%) responded significantly to opponents' displayed histories.
- Responses grew with the evidence: per SD of k1 payoff, k1-consistency rose 1.6 pp against opponents with fewer than 14 games and 14 pp against opponents with more than 46 games.
EFFECT: - 18% non-uniform in frequencies vs 47% reacting to information.
- Reaction size scales about 9× with the length of the opponent's history.
IMPLICATION: - Against experienced opponents, static hand-frequency leans (C06) have little to exploit.
- Read how opponents react to information: your visible history and previous outcomes.
- The longer opponents can watch you, the more they will react, so vary your visible tendencies.

[MF7] conf=high
STATEMENT: **Efficient partial exploitation** (my derivation). Against an opponent who over-plays one hand X while the other two are balanced, the most efficient partial exploitation keeps X itself at 1/3 and shifts probability from the hand X beats to the hand that beats X.

Against a 36/32/32 Rock-leaning opponent, the mix R 1/3, P 1/3+ε, S 1/3−ε earns 0.08ε of expected gain at a worst-case loss of ε:
- 33/58/8: +2.0 pp at −25 pp.
- 33/67/0: +2.67 pp at −33 pp.

That is twice the efficiency of the symmetric leans 50/25/25 (+1.0 pp at −25) and 40/30/30 (+0.4 pp at −10). For small leans the trade-off is linear, not 'highly concave'.
EFFECT: Twice the gain per unit of worst-case exposure compared with symmetric leans; absolute gains are at most +2–4 pp per throw.
IMPLICATION: - If you lean at all against a 'Rock-heavy' opponent, take Scissors out and add Paper; do not cut Rock.
- In general: drop the hand their favourite beats, add the hand that beats their favourite, and keep their favourite hand at 1/3.
CAVEATS: - Assumes the estimated bias is real; real biases are small (1–3 pp) and noisy.
- The worst case assumes an opponent who reads your lean.
