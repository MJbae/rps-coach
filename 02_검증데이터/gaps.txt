#### GAP: Korean format variants (가위바위보 하나빼기, i.e. 'rock-paper-scissors minus one', and 묵찌빠): how should they be played? The verified set covers standard RPS only.
WHY: Korean team events may use 하나빼기 (shown in Squid Game season 2; Namu Wiki: '오징어 게임(시즌 2)에서 하나빼기 버전이 등장한다'). 묵찌빠 is a common Korean decider. In both, the equilibrium differs from 1/3-1/3-1/3:
- In 하나빼기, the pair you show first can make you a +1/3 favourite or underdog.
- In 묵찌빠, winning the opening throw makes you a 2/3 favourite.

Applying standard-RPS advice to these games would be wrong.

  [G4-F1] conf=high
  STATEMENT: 하나빼기 (show two hands, then withdraw one) has an exact solution.

Stage 1, choosing a pair:
- Show two different hands. Showing the same hand twice is weakly dominated.
- The three pairs form their own RPS cycle, each beating the next by +1/3: {Rock+Paper} beats {Rock+Scissors}, which beats {Paper+Scissors}, which beats {Rock+Paper}.
- So choose the pair uniformly at random.

Stage 2, keeping a hand:
- Same pair on both sides: keep the hand that wins inside the pair (Rock+Paper -> Paper; Rock+Scissors -> Rock; Paper+Scissors -> Scissors). The result is a tie.
- Different pairs (they always share exactly one hand): each player keeps the shared hand with probability 2/3 and the other hand with 1/3.
- The favourite is the player whose extra hand beats the shared hand. The favourite wins 4/9, ties 4/9 and loses 1/9 (+1/3 per game).

Example: you show Paper+Scissors and the opponent shows Paper+Rock, so Paper is shared and your Scissors beats it. You are favoured. You keep Paper 2/3 and Scissors 1/3; they should keep Paper 2/3 and Rock 1/3.
  DESIGN: Game-theoretic analysis from math blog and organisation pages, plus my exact computation. No human data.
  EFFECT: In stage 2 the favoured pair wins 44.4%, ties 44.4% and loses 11.1% (+33.3 pp net). The game value is 0 when stage 1 is randomized.
  IMPLICATION: - Pick your pair with a device: uniform over the three pairs.
- After seeing both pairs: if they are the same, keep the hand that wins inside the pair. If they differ, keep the shared hand 2/3 of the time (e.g., with a die: 1-4 keep the shared hand, 5-6 keep the other).
- A human bias toward the 'safe' shared hand, or toward 'my extra hand', can be exploited.
  CAVEATS: - Assumes zero-sum scoring with ties replayed.
- The POST (Pe-Aguirre) argues that mutual 'loss aversion' in Squid Game's lethal setting means perfect play 'always results in a draw'. That framing does not apply to a tournament.

If both players throw uniformly at random in each exchange, the attacker's win probability V satisfies V = 1/3 + V/3 + (1-V)/3, so V = 2/3. Uniform play is an equilibrium: the attacker's payoff matrix for an exchange is circulant, so every pure choice earns 2/3 against a uniform opponent.

#### CONTRADICTIONS

- **After a tie (the most consequential conflict).**
- Zhejiang a=2 data (A1/C08): opponents repeat the tied hand 43% of the time, so beat it (Rock-Rock -> Paper, +0.20).
- Fixed-opponent data with no tie reward point the other way: throw the hand the tied hand beats. Western pairs (MF01/MF7) give +0.05 to +0.13. The new 500-player Japanese data give +0.084 overall and +0.114 in throws 2-10, while 'beat the tied hand' scores -0.14 to +0.01. Ogawa's classroom null (C13) was confounded by its comparison.
- Reconcile: the Zhejiang tie response tracks the tie payoff. Repeat rates fall from 0.80 when a tie pays like a win to 0.34 when ties pay 0 (MF02), alongside random re-matching and long 40-s sessions.
- In a knockout replay the tie is worth nothing and the opponent is fixed, so default to Rock-Rock -> Scissors, Paper-Paper -> Rock, Scissors-Scissors -> Paper. Only after a second identical tie does the evidence favour beating the tied hand (39.7% third repeat in Japan).

- **Win-stay and relay champions.**
- One side: Angle C (C15) and Angle E (C15) imply 'a winner repeats, so throw what beats their winning hand'. Support: Zhejiang 0.528, Zhejiang vs AI 0.469, Dyson computer labs 0.36-0.44, relay computer avatars 38.2%.
- Other side: human fixed pairs (US 0.322, Australia 0.262) and the Japanese sample (0.305) show no win-stay. There, 'beat the winner's hand' loses (Australia -0.110, Japan -0.047).
- Reconcile by setting: win-stay appears with computer avatars and in reward-heavy or re-matched labs, and grows late in long sessions (MF2). Against people in a fixed bout, the safer reply after an opponent's win is the hand their winning hand beats (Japan +0.061, Australia +0.103).

- **Over-switching vs over-repeating.**
- Angle C C09/C10 (tennis serves, random-generation tasks): humans switch too often.
- Angle C MF2 and Angle A A1 (Zhejiang): repeats exceed each type of switch.
- The new data side with over-switching in fixed-opponent play across four countries including Japan: repeat 28.2% overall; 0.265-0.298 in throws 2-10 for Japan, Australia and the US. Japanese field data show 22.8%.
- Reconcile: the repeat-prone samples are the Zhejiang lab protocols plus Eyler's single cell, which is known only second-hand. They do not show that East Asians in general repeat.

- **Opening throw.** The candidates conflict:
- 'Paper first': Yoshizawa; Angle C C06, +2.8 pp against Facebook openers.
- 'Scissors against experienced players': Batzilis.
- US students open Paper-heavy (42.7%), so Scissors gains +15.6 pp.
- Japanese players open Paper-poor (20.4% observed, 20.6% self-reported), so Rock gains +18.8 pp.
- Moerel's first games are Scissors-heavy (46.9%).
- Ogawa found no first-throw effect, and a Korean wiki claims Koreans open with Paper.

Reconcile: openers depend on population and context, and the best opener even flips sign across samples. Without Korean data, randomize the opener and scout opponents' first throws.

- **Rock excess and 'always Paper'.**
- Rock excess: screen labs (Zhejiang 35.7%, Moerel 35.3%, Dyson 35.6%) and Japanese field tallies (35.0%).
- No Rock excess in live refereed play: UK 32.4%, Japan 33.1%; Hungary shows Scissors 36.7%.
- Scissors excess in some screen samples: US dyads 34.6%; Japanese Komai 35.1%, where 'always Paper' scores -2.9 pp.

Reconcile by interface (Zhejiang's Rock button was left-most), live fist-pumping, and population. Any fixed hand default is worth about 3 pp at most and has the wrong sign in several samples.

- **Short horizon.**
- MF2 (Angle A) concluded the first 1-20 throws are close to random.
- Pooled over outcomes, repeat-aversion is present from the second throw. 'Always X-' in throws 2-10: Japan +0.098 (SE 0.017, n=500), Australia +0.095, US +0.059.

Reconcile: MF2's outcome-split early cells were small (about 360-750 transitions). What builds late in a session is the Zhejiang-vs-AI win-stay, not repeat-aversion.

- **Imitation.**
- Cook 2012: +3.0 pp more draws, one-tailed p about 0.055.
- Null replications: Aczel 2012 (Hungary, -1.2 pp); Ito 2017 (Japan, +1.7 pp, n.s.). Pooled g=0.15 with a CI that includes 0.
- In the two-gesture game, the effect is tiny and appears only early (Belot 2013).

Reconcile: small samples and differing procedures. Treat imitation as having no reliable effect.

- **Stakes and win-stay.**
- Wang & Xu: win-stay falls as the win/tie payoff ratio rises (rho = -0.29).
- Forder & Dyson: win-stay rises from 35.9% to 48.4% when wins are worth +2.

Reconcile: the manipulations differ (relative tie value vs win magnitude), as do the designs (between vs within subjects) and the opponents (humans vs computer). Do not predict opponents' win-stay from tournament stakes.

- **Direction after a loss.** Popular media (NPR, MIT Technology Review 2014) said losers move Rock -> Paper. The data mostly show the reverse: players who lose downgrade (Rock -> Scissors) more often than they upgrade.
- Zhejiang: 0.365 vs 0.276.
- Dyson 2016: 0.405 vs 0.372.
- Australia: 0.374 vs 0.340.
- Japan: 0.399 vs 0.336.
- Near-equal in the US (0.386 vs 0.377) and Forder & Dyson (39.41% vs 39.13%).

Reconcile: the media misread 'clockwise'. Downgrading is the most common post-loss switch.

- **Fixed rules vs exploitability.**
- Angle A C2 recommends outcome-specific rules, and MF1 warns that 'throw what your own last hand beats' is easily exploited (humans won 62% against such a bot over 300 rounds).
- The new time course shows humans need about 7-10 throws to read even a 90% habit (throws 1-3: +0.016, n.s.).

Reconcile by horizon: rule-based leans are low-risk inside one short bout but risky across rematches or when opponents are watching.

- **Team deliberation.**
- Angle E C11: teams are more game-theoretic.
- MF5: that advantage is confined to dominance-solvable games.
- Okano: teams are closer to minimax in zero-sum games, but the advantage fades over time.

Reconcile: in RPS, team discussion helps balance and randomize play, not find edges.

- **Pausing after a loss.**
- Correlations: Dyson 2021, r=0.202 (reported p=.036; about .089 two-tailed when recomputed); Dyson 2018, r about .58-.65.
- The only causal test, a forced pause, cut lose-shift by 7.7 pp but produced the lowest win rate (33.5%).

Reconcile: slowing down marks good modelling of the opponent; it does not cause winning.

- **Doubled hands.** The WRPS 'Double Run' rule says that after X, X you can eliminate X. The data disagree: a third identical throw followed 28.5% (Australia), 30.5% (Dyson), 34.0% (US) and 35.8% (Japan) of the time, and 39.7% after two same-hand ties in Japan.

Reconcile: never eliminate a doubled hand; players who have already repeated are prone to repeat.

- **Gender.**
- Claims: WRPS 'males lead with Rock' and the Japanese self-report (women Scissors 46.9%, men Rock 42.4%).
- Observed Australian play: no overall gender difference (Rock 35.0% for men vs 35.7% for women). Opener directions match the claims but are not significant (women Scissors 53%, men Rock 41%; p=0.18).

Reconcile: gender is at most a weak hint about openers.
