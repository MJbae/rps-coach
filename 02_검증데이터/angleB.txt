In every fixed-opponent dataset (Dyson 2016 Canada, Forder 2016 UK, Moerel 2025 Australia, Brockbank & Vul 2020 USA), throwing the hand the tied hand beats nets +5 to +11 pp, and throwing the hand that beats the tied hand nets −3 to −10 pp. The reverse holds only in Chinese random-rematch labs where ties earn points (a=2: about +20 pp for the hand that beats the tied hand). When ties are worth nothing (a=∞), both switches are mildly positive (+4 to +6 pp). Yoshizawa's Japanese field data measure overall repeats (22.75%), not after-tie behaviour.
DESIGN: Derived. Combines the designs listed in C01, C04, C05, C07, C08, C11, C14 and C16.
EFFECT: Paper default: +2.8 to +4.9 pp per throw in Rock-biased datasets; −0.2 to −4.6 pp in others. After-tie conditional edges: about +8 to +20 pp, but with opposite optimal moves across populations.
IMPLICATION: A blind 'always Paper' rule gives at best a few percent. Larger edges need population-specific, outcome-conditioned reads: after-tie behaviour, repeat versus avoid, and continued cycling. These must be checked against the actual opponent, because published populations disagree on their direction.
CAVEATS: Values are computed from heterogeneous designs (screen vs real hands, computer vs human opponents, incentives) and are not independent across Zhejiang datasets. Edges are relative to marginal frequencies and ignore the opponent's own adaptation.
RELIABILITY: Derived synthesis across heterogeneous designs. Arithmetic is correct. The main correction comes from adding two large public raw datasets of human-vs-human play with fixed opponents.

STATEMENT: In fixed-opponent human-vs-human play, the opponent repeats the tied hand least often after a tie. Net for throwing the hand X beats: +5.4 pp (95% CI 1.2 to 9.7); for throwing the hand that beats X: −9.8 pp (−14.0 to −5.3). Brockbank & Vul 2020 raw data (USA, 58 fixed dyads, 11,656 ties): 29.1/35.2/35.6%, with nets of +5.8 pp (2.4 to 9.1) and −6.3 pp (−10.6 to −1.7). By tied hand, 'throw the hand X beats' was positive in all 9 hand × dataset cells across Moerel, Brockbank and Dyson 2016 (+1.0 to +16.8 pp). 'Throw the hand that beats X' was negative in 8 of 9.

STATEMENT: The Chinese 'repeat after a tie' pattern depends on the payoff. In Wang & Xu 2014 Fig. 7 (504 Zhejiang students, 72 per treatment; my digitisation), the share repeating the tied hand fell steadily as the tie's value shrank: 0.80 when a tie paid as much as a win (a=1), then 0.62 (a=1.1), 0.43 (a=2), 0.39 (a=4), 0.36 (a=9), 0.36 (a=100), and 0.34 when ties paid nothing (a=∞). At a=∞ the most common response was to switch to the hand that beats the tied hand (0.38). Switching to the hand the tied hand beats was the least common move in every treatment. Consistently, Dyson 2022 (Canada, against a random computer) found draw-stay of about 30–31% when draws scored 0 or +1 (Exp. 1, n=40). In Exp. 2 (n=36), draw-stay was 36% at +1 vs 26% at −1 (draw-shift 63.98% vs 73.73%, Tukey p<.05).
DESIGN: Wang & Xu: incentivised lab at Zhejiang University; 6-player groups re-matched at random every round; 300 rounds; seven payoff treatments (win=a, tie=1, loss=0; a=∞ means ties pay 0). Dyson 2022: University of Alberta undergraduates (course credit); 3 blocks of 120 trials against a computer that played each item 40 times per block; draw value manip
EFFECT: T0 falls 46 pp from a=1 to a=∞ (Spearman rho −0.531, n=504). At a=∞, best responses to the tied hand X are: the hand that beats X +5.5 pp, the hand X beats +3.9 pp, repeat X −9.4 pp. At a=2: +20.2, −9.8 and −10.2 pp.

[MF03] conf=medium src=['M02', 'M03', 'M01', 'S11', 'M04']
STATEMENT: The Rock excess is not universal, even in large human-vs-human datasets. Brockbank & Vul 2020 (USA, 58 stable online dyads, 34,557 throws in complete games; my computation): Rock 31.9% (95% CI 30.7–33.0), Paper 33.6%, Scissors 34.6% (33.6–35.6). Always throwing Paper would have lost 2.7 pp per throw (CI −4.8 to −0.7). Moerel 2025 (Australia, 31 fixed pairs, 29,511 throws, option order randomised per block): Rock 35.3% (34.4–36.4), Paper 32.5%, Scissors 32.1%, so always-Paper gained +3.2 pp (1.4–5.2). Dyson 2022 (Canada, against a computer): Rock 34.8–37.0% across conditions.
DESIGN: As in MF01 for Brockbank and Moerel. Dyson 2022: 40 undergraduates against a computer, course credit.
EFFECT: Expected value of a Paper default ranges from −2.7 pp (US dyads) to +3.2 pp (Australian pairs); both CIs exclude 0.
IMPLICATION: A blanket 'Paper is safe' default is worth at most about 3 pp and can be negative. Moerel's randomised-position data show the Rock excess is not just a screen artefact, but the US dyads show the opposite. Rely on observed opponent tendencies rather than a population prior.
CAVEATS: Online or computerised play. The on-screen card order in Brockbank is not reported. Neither dataset is East Asian.

STATEMENT: Opening-throw distributions are unstable across samples, so no opener is reliably favoured. In the first round of the Brockbank & Vul dyads (n=120), openers were Paper 44.2%, Rock 33.3%, Scissors 22.5% (χ² p=0.015). In the first game of the Moerel pairs (n=49 valid), they were Scissors 46.9%, Rock 28.6%, Paper 24.5% (p=0.12). Compare Eyler (n=119): Rock 55.5%. Batzilis (5.0M match openers): Paper 34.8 / Rock 34.0 / Scissors 31.2. Japanese self-report (n=1,000): Scissors 42.0 / Rock 37.4 / Paper 20.6. Japanese children's first throws (Uchida): Scissors > Paper > Rock.
DESIGN: First trial of long computerised human-pair sessions (US online, Australian lab), compared with other designs.
EFFECT: Expected value of opening with Paper: −18.3 pp (Moerel), +10.8 (Brockbank), +43.7 (Eyler), +2.8 (Batzilis), −4.6 (Japanese stated).
IMPLICATION: Treat the first throw as close to random. Any 'opener rule' is specific to one population. A first-throw edge needs scouting of the actual opponent pool.
CAVEATS: Small n for first trials. The first trial of a computerised session may not reflect a one-shot live opener.

[MF05] conf=medium src=['M01', 'M02', 'M03', 'S05']
STATEMENT: Persistence of cycling replicates in human-vs-human data. After an upgrade (e.g., Rock→Paper), the next move was another upgrade 43.1% of the time in Moerel (n=10,049) and 46.6% in Brockbank (n=12,455). After a downgrade (e.g., Rock→Scissors), another downgrade followed 44.8% and 45.2% of the time. Dyson 2016 reported 43.5% and 42.4%. A third identical throw after two followed 28.5% (Moerel, n=7,970) and 34.0% (Brockbank, n=10,060) of the time, so the repeated hand is not eliminated.
DESIGN: As in MF01, with sequences pooled within players.
EFFECT: Continued cycling runs +10 to +13 pp above 33.3%. A third identical throw runs −4.8 to +0.7 pp relative to 33.3%.
IMPLICATION: After an opponent goes Rock→Paper, expect Scissors and throw Rock. After Rock→Scissors, expect Paper and throw Scissors. After X, X, do not treat X as eliminated (28–34%), contrary to WRPS 'Double Run'.
CAVEATS: Pooled across players, so part of the effect reflects stable individual preferences for one step direction. Computerised play.

[MF06] conf=medium src=['M01', 'S11', 'M02', 'M03', 'S09', 'S06']
STATEMENT: Below-chance repetition is the norm against a fixed human opponent, after every outcome, including wins. In Moerel's raw data, overall repeat was 27.5% (50 of 62 players below 1/3): 26.1% after a win, 28.1% after a loss, 28.3% after a tie. In Brockbank's, it was 28.2% (88 of 124 below 1/3): 32.1% after a win, 23.5% after a loss, 29.1% after a tie. A simple rule ('throw the hand the opponent's last throw beats') netted +7.3 pp (Moerel, n=28,978) and +7.9 pp (Brockbank, n=34,557). The opposite rule ('throw the hand that beats their last throw') netted −10.2 and −7.3 pp. By contrast, the Chinese random-rematch lab shows repeating above chance (stay 0.376–0.531 for a ≥ 1.1). There the opposite rule wins: +15.8 pp at a=2, from Wang & Xu Table 2.
DESIGN: As in MF01. Wang & Xu: 6-player groups with random re-matching. Forder: UK students against a computer.
EFFECT: Exploiting repeat-avoidance in fixed human pairs: +7.3 to +7.9 pp per throw. In random-rematch Chinese data the sign reverses (about ±16 pp at a=2).
IMPLICATION: In a bout against one opponent, the hand they just threw is their least likely next throw, even after they win. Favour the hand their last throw beats, and verify with the opponent's first few transitions. The Chinese win-stay / tie-stay data come from re-shuffled opponents and should not be the default for a fixed bout.
CAVEATS: Computerised Western samples. The rule's edge would shrink against an opponent who notices it. Results from re-shuffled designs differ sharply, which suggests the opponent structure matters (Brockbank & Vul 2020 make the same point).

STATEMENT: Japanese stated after-tie habits show strong repeat-avoidance with a downgrade preference. STATEMENT: Ties are common in live real-hand play, and the 'automatic imitation' boost to ties did not replicate. CAVEATS: Small numbers of independent triads; blindfolds and spoken announcements make play unnatural; European samples.
